# v0.1 Initial Ten-Case Audit

## Status

The first 10 cases are a purposive **schema-testing sample**, not a representative sample of Indian social-engineering cybercrime.

## What the pilot successfully captured

The initial cases span:

- digital-arrest impersonation
- fake-investment social proof
- work-from-home/task fraud
- family-member impersonation
- fake customer-care malware delivery
- cross-border tech-support impersonation
- matrimonial/romance impersonation
- phishing combined with vishing
- compromised-account/superior impersonation
- sextortion and pseudo-legal coercion

The framework therefore survived contact with substantially different social-engineering patterns.

## Taxonomy changes required

Two categories were added during coding:

- `family_impersonation`
- `sextortion`

This is expected behaviour in a methodology pilot.

## Strong recurring pattern

A central distinction repeatedly appears between:

1. the **social-engineering operator**, and
2. downstream people associated with bank accounts, SIMs, cash withdrawals, devices, or communications.

The public record can sometimes strongly support the second while leaving the first unresolved.

This validates the project's decision not to collapse artefact association into human attribution.

## Evidence pattern

Across the pilot, public judicial material often contains substantial discussion of:

- bank-account trails
- withdrawals and cash-out
- CDR/telecom links
- seized devices and SIMs
- chat or WhatsApp material
- CCTV in some cash-out investigations

By contrast, the public record less consistently exposes:

- platform-provider account records
- authenticated provider-side metadata
- complete forensic extraction details
- chain-of-custody discussion
- technical provenance of screenshots/messages

This may become a research question rather than merely missing data.

## Schema pressure points

### 1. One incident row can contain several actors

`attribution_target` sometimes has to summarise different roles in a network. A future version may need an actor-level companion table.

### 2. Network-wide and victim-specific numbers can differ

For example, one court case may describe a single victim's loss while also revealing a larger set of victims, bank accounts, or devices. Future versions may benefit from explicit field scope.

### 3. Procedural posture strongly affects certainty

Bail orders are rich sources but usually report prosecution and defence positions before final adjudication. Source-stage coding is therefore essential.

### 4. Mechanism vocabulary may need expansion

The ten cases reveal mechanisms such as social proof, legitimacy theatre, emotional emergency, relationship grooming, search-result trust, small initial rewards, authority, shame, and isolation. The present boolean mechanism fields capture the broad categories, but later versions may need a separate mechanism table.

## Release review status

The source-by-source pre-publication review was completed on 16 August 2026. Corrections are documented in `docs/v0.1_release_audit.md`.

The ten records are now coded as `reviewed`.

## No prevalence claims

Do not use the ten cases to claim which scam type is "most common", which state has the most incidents, average Indian loss, or any population-level pattern.
