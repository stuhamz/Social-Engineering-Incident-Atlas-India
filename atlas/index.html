What do court records really tell us about a cybercrime incident?

The Atlas brings together 300 documented incidents involving social engineering in India. It records how deception is described in publicly available material and distinguishes evidence of accounts, devices or payments from evidence identifying the people behind an interaction.

This is a research corpus, not a count of all cybercrime in India or a list of people proven guilty. Case descriptions and coding reflect available records, which may report allegations or interim findings rather than final convictions.

Who is this for?

Researchers, journalists and students examining social engineering, documentary evidence and legal attribution. The index helps you find case IDs, attack categories and the type of source used.

For complete research notes, coding decisions and registered source references, use the case-note directory and methodology.

What is the central question?

When a judgment describes a bank account, a phone number, a device or a platform profile, what does that actually establish about a particular person's actions?

The Atlas supports exploration of this question; a focused academic study of judicial reasoning requires its own judgment-level reading and evidence checks.

How to read and cite the evidence

The Atlas is purposively assembled, not representative of reported cybercrime nationwide. T1/T2/T3 source types are research classifications, not proof that an original court document was independently authenticated. Automated dataset checks do not constitute independent scholarly review.
